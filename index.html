IN PLAIN WORDS
About 1 in 3 adults sleeps less than 7 hours. Those people feel more tired during the day. When people who sleep too little get more sleep, their fatigue goes down and their energy goes up. Sleeping more than you need does not add extra energy.

And the other way round — adding sleep

Athletes who slept +111 min per night for 5–7 weeks: fatigue ↓, vigour ↑ (P < 0.001).

Yes. Cutting sleep causes a large drop in positive mood and energy. Extending sleep in people who sleep too little lowers fatigue and raises vigour. (Tomaso et al., 2021; Mah et al., 2011; Kamdar et al., 2004)

Who benefits — by starting sleep duration

Short sleepers (< 7 h)

Biggest gain

Sleeping 1–2 h more reduced fatigue and raised vigour in trials (Kamdar et al., 2004; Mah et al., 2011).

Normal sleepers (7–9 h)

Small gain

A week of 10 h in bed lowered fatigue a little, but sleepiness and mood did not change (Mantua et al., 2019).

Long sleepers (≥ 9 h)

No expected gain

More is not better: long sleep is linked to higher all-cause mortality, HR 1.29 (Gu et al., 2024). Check for other causes of tiredness.

Strength of evidence (GRADE)

Rated with the GRADE domains: study design, risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision (Guyatt et al., 2008).

High
Losing sleep lowers energy and positive mood — 154 experiments, N = 5,717 (Palmer et al., 2024; Tomaso et al., 2021)
Moderate
Short sleepers who extend sleep feel less fatigued and more vigorous — small trials, consistent results (Kamdar et al., 2004; Mah et al., 2011)
Low / none
No real energy boost expected from extra sleep if you already sleep 7–9 h (Mantua et al., 2019)
Caution
≥ 9 h is linked to higher mortality, HR 1.29 (Gu et al., 2024); people underrate how impaired they are after 2 weeks at 6 h (Van Dongen et al., 2003)
Track your sleep first, aim for 7–9 h, let the data decide.
